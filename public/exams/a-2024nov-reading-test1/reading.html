Venice and the Rising Tide: Engineering Against Nature

Venice, often referred to as “La Serenissima,” has long captivated the imagination of travelers with its winding canals, gondola-laden waterways, and centuries-old architecture. But beneath the charm lies a centuries-old problem that modern science is racing to solve: the threat of flooding. Built on a fragile network of wooden piles in a shallow lagoon on Italy’s northeastern coast, the city has endured periodic flooding throughout its history. However, the nature and frequency of these events have changed dramatically in recent decades, prompting urgent intervention.
Historically, acqua alta, or high water, would flood parts of the city a few times a year, typically during autumn and winter when tides were at their peak. Residents accepted this as part of Venetian life. Raised walkways were routinely erected, and shopkeepers kept waterproof barriers at the ready. But what was once a manageable inconvenience has become a dangerous and increasingly frequent crisis. Between the 1950s and 1970s, Venice experienced a few dozen floods per decade. Today, it experiences dozens annually. In 2019 alone, a particularly catastrophic event submerged over 80% of the city, causing immense damage to historical buildings, shops, and infrastructure.

Several factors contribute to this worsening scenario. One of the most prominent is subsidence, a natural process where the ground gradually sinks. In Venice’s case, subsidence has been exacerbated by groundwater extraction in the 20th century. Combined with eustatic sea-level rise—a consequence of global warming—the city is now more vulnerable than ever. Climate scientists estimate that sea levels in the northern Adriatic may rise by up to one meter by the end of the 21st century, which could render parts of Venice permanently underwater.
In response to this dire outlook, Italian authorities initiated one of the most ambitious civil engineering projects in Europe: the MOSE project (Modulo Sperimentale Elettromeccanico). First conceptualized in the 1980s, MOSE aims to protect Venice and its lagoon from tides of up to three meters. The system consists of 78 mobile gates installed at the three inlets (Lido, Malamocco, and Chioggia) that connect the Adriatic Sea to the Venetian Lagoon. These barriers rest on the seabed during normal conditions and are filled with compressed air to rise when tides exceed a predetermined threshold, effectively sealing the lagoon off from the sea.

The project is a feat of modern engineering. Each gate is approximately 30 meters wide and 20 meters high, built to withstand immense hydrodynamic pressure. The barriers can be raised in about 30 minutes and lowered when the danger has passed. Unlike permanent barriers, MOSE’s mobile design is intended to preserve the natural tidal flow, which is essential for the ecological health of the lagoon and its resident species.

Despite its technological sophistication, MOSE has not been without controversy. Construction, which began in 2003, was plagued by repeated delays, financial mismanagement, and political scandal. Costs ballooned from initial estimates of around €1.6 billion to over €5 billion. Critics accused government agencies and contractors of corruption, with several officials arrested in 2014 for this as part of a broader investigation into misuse of public funds. These scandals not only slowed the project but also eroded public trust in its effectiveness.
Furthermore, environmentalists and marine scientists have raised concerns about MOSE’s long-term impacts. Repeatedly closing off the lagoon could alter sediment flows, increase water stagnation, and disrupt delicate ecosystems. Some researchers also argue that MOSE is a temporary solution; it was designed based on historical data and might not be able to handle the most extreme sea-level rise scenarios projected for the end of the century. There is also the issue of maintenance. The underwater gates are subject to corrosion and biological growth, requiring frequent and costly upkeep.

Nonetheless, MOSE achieved a significant milestone in October 2020 when it was successfully activated to prevent flooding during a high tide. For the first time in decades, Venice’s iconic St. Mark’s Square remained dry during an acqua alta event. Since then, the system has been deployed on numerous occasions, with largely positive results. While not flawless, its performance has been viewed as a promising start and a vital step toward safeguarding Venice’s cultural and architectural heritage.
Looking forward, experts suggest that MOSE should not be seen as a singular solution but as part of a multi-pronged strategy. Additional measures such as strengthening the city’s foundations, redesigning drainage systems, promoting sustainable tourism, and implementing climate change mitigation policies are equally crucial. Urban planners are also examining adaptive strategies like "living shorelines" and floating infrastructure that may one day become necessary in a world where sea levels continue to rise.

Ultimately, Venice represents a broader challenge faced by coastal cities worldwide. From Jakarta to Miami, the intersection of urban development and environmental vulnerability calls for innovative and integrated responses. MOSE, for all its imperfections, demonstrates that even the most ancient cities can find modern means of survival—if vision, funding, and cooperation align.

THE EMERGENCE OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

A. The concept of artificial intelligence (AI) has its origins in the mid-20th century when scientists began to explore the possibility of creating machines capable of simulating human thought processes. This idea gained traction as researchers recognized that computers, like the human brain, could process information and make decisions. A pivotal moment in the development of AI occurred in 1950 when British mathematician and computer scientist Alan Turing devised a test to determine whether a machine could exhibit human-like intelligence. In this test, a human participant would engage in a text-based conversation with both another human and a computer. If the participant was unable to distinguish between the two, the machine was deemed to have passed the test. The term 'artificial intelligence' was officially introduced in 1956 by American computer scientist John McCarthy at a conference in the United States, marking the formal inception of AI as a field of study.

B. Interest in AI surged during the 1970s and 1980s, prompting substantial financial investment from governments in the United Kingdom and the United States. Researchers envisioned AI systems that could mimic human reasoning, leading to ambitious predictions about the capabilities of intelligent machines. However, by the late 1980s, optimism waned as AI failed to develop at the pace from initial predictions. One of the primary obstacles was the limited capacity of computers to store and process large volumes of data efficiently. At the time, hardware limitations and computational constraints made it difficult for AI to achieve significant breakthroughs. As a result, funding for AI research declined, leading to a period often referred to as the 'AI winter.' This downturn lasted for several years, slowing advancements in the field and discouraging further investment.

C. Nevertheless, this period of stagnation was short-lived. By the 1990s, advancements in computing power and the increasing availability of data rekindled progress in AI research. The exponential growth in data storage capabilities, coupled with faster data processing speeds, allowed researchers to develop more sophisticated algorithms. A significant milestone was reached in 1997 when IBM’s Deep Blue, a computer program, defeated reigning world chess champion Garry Kasparov. This event demonstrated AI’s ability to outperform human intellect in highly complex tasks. The victory of Deep Blue challenged previous assumptions about the limitations of machine intelligence and signaled a turning point in AI research. This success paved the way for further exploration into AI’s potential applications beyond board games, extending into real-world problem-solving scenarios.

D. Since then, AI has developed rapidly, becoming an integral part of various industries. Today, AI is capable of performing an array of tasks, from making phone calls to scheduling appointments and even operating vehicles autonomously. It is widely employed across sectors such as finance and healthcare, where it is used to detect fraudulent transactions and assist in medical diagnoses. AI-driven technologies also play a role in facial recognition, language translation, and personalized recommendations for entertainment and consumer products. Moreover, AI is increasingly being utilized in predicting natural disasters, further illustrating its expanding influence on modern society. Governments and businesses alike are recognizing the transformative potential of AI, investing heavily in research and development to harness its capabilities for practical applications.

E. The underlying mechanism of AI has evolved significantly over time. Early AI systems relied on predefined rules created by human programmers, instructing machines on how to respond to specific situations. However, such systems struggled when faced with unpredictable scenarios. Modern AI, in contrast, is predominantly based on data-driven learning. By analyzing vast amounts of data, AI systems identify patterns and improve their accuracy over time. This approach, known as machine learning, enables AI to perform tasks such as image recognition. For example, an AI system can be trained to recognize dogs by being exposed to numerous images of them. Over time, it learns to identify a dog even in images it has never encountered before. This ability to learn from data rather than relying on fixed instructions makes modern AI systems far more adaptable and efficient than their predecessors.

F. Although machine learning has existed for decades, it has only recently become highly effective due to the increased computational power and the availability of large datasets. The effectiveness of machine learning relies on data: the more data it processes, the more accurate it becomes. This is one of the key reasons why AI has advanced so rapidly and is now capable of tasks that were previously thought to be exclusive to human cognition. AI's ability to detect patterns that might escape human perception has made it invaluable across a wide range of applications. From self-driving cars that analyze vast amounts of real-time data to virtual assistants that understand and respond to human speech, AI continues to revolutionize the way humans interact with technology.

G. The future of AI is both promising and uncertain. It is expected to continue transforming the way people live and work, offering solutions to complex problems and improving decision-making processes. However, AI also presents significant challenges. Concerns have been raised about its potential misuse, such as the development of autonomous weapons or its role in undermining privacy. Additionally, the increasing reliance on AI-driven decision-making raises ethical questions regarding fairness and transparency. The use of AI in hiring processes, legal decisions, and even policing has sparked debates about bias and accountability. As AI continues to evolve, it is crucial to ensure that its implementation aligns with ethical principles and serves the broader interests of society. Governments, researchers, and industry leaders must work together to establish policies and regulations that promote responsible AI development while safeguarding human rights and freedoms. Striking a balance between innovation and ethical considerations will be essential in shaping the future of artificial intelligence.

A. The term ‘sharing economy’ refers to a modern method of exchanging goods and services that deviates from traditional ownership-based business models. At its core, the sharing economy enables individuals to monetise their underused assets—such as homes, cars, or even skills—by making them available to others, typically through digital platforms. This approach is reshaping various sectors by encouraging access over ownership and facilitating peer-to-peer transactions at a scale previously unimaginable.

B. The rise of the sharing economy has been driven by the rapid evolution of the internet. During the 1990s, the internet primarily connected computers; by the early 2000s, it was connecting people. Today, with the advent of platforms like Uber and Airbnb, it connects possessions—transforming the way individuals interact with goods and services. These platforms serve as intermediaries, allowing private individuals to share their resources with users willing to pay for short-term use.

C. This model is having a profound impact on heavily regulated sectors such as transportation, accommodation, finance, and food services. These industries have long relied on strict legal frameworks to limit competition and ensure safety or quality. However, by enabling ordinary people to provide services outside of traditional structures, the sharing economy is creating alternative markets and challenging the dominance of long-established businesses.

D. Airbnb and Uber are two of the most prominent examples of this trend. Airbnb began in 2008 as a small startup offering accommodation in private homes and has since grown into a platform with over 1.5 million listings, surpassing many global hotel chains in scale. In some cities, its listings outnumber the top ten hotel chains combined. Uber, launched in 2009, allows users to book rides through a smartphone app. Its model has disrupted traditional taxi services across nearly 400 cities worldwide. The surge in ride-sharing has significantly reduced the value of taxi medallions—licenses once worth hundreds of thousands of dollars—causing financial distress for drivers and lenders alike.

E. Beyond Uber and Airbnb, the sharing economy encompasses a growing number of companies across various sectors. Feastly, for example, allows amateur chefs to host paid meals in their homes, turning domestic kitchens into temporary restaurants. Lending Club connects borrowers directly with individual investors, bypassing banks. Another example is WeWork, which leases large office spaces and rents them to freelancers, startups, and small businesses on flexible terms. It has become Manhattan’s largest commercial tenant, occupying millions of square feet of office space.

F. Proponents of the sharing economy argue that it fosters innovation, creates economic opportunities, and increases access to services. They claim that it helps people supplement their income by offering spare rooms, unused vehicles, or professional skills, while simultaneously providing consumers with more affordable and flexible alternatives. Supporters also see the model as a way to reduce waste by making better use of existing resources.

G. However, critics raise serious concerns. They argue that the sharing economy undermines labour protections, replaces secure employment with gig-based work, and skirts regulations designed to protect consumers and workers. For example, Uber drivers are often classified as independent contractors rather than employees, meaning they do not receive benefits such as health insurance or paid leave. Similarly, short-term rentals through Airbnb have been blamed for inflating housing costs in some cities by reducing the supply of long-term rentals.

H. Regulatory bodies have found it difficult to keep pace with the rapid growth of these platforms. In some regions, governments have banned sharing-economy companies outright, claiming that their operations violate local laws or pose safety risks. In other cases, cities and countries have attempted to develop new rules, only to find that enforcement is challenging or that companies quickly adapt to circumvent restrictions.

I. In the United States, the sharing economy has sparked political debate. Some lawmakers argue that it represents a valuable source of innovation and job creation, while others believe it should be more tightly controlled. In New York, for instance, state authorities have investigated Airbnb for violating regulations related to short-term rentals. In San Francisco, new legislation requires Uber drivers to obtain business licences and pay local taxes, though the company has pushed back against enforcement efforts.

J. As the sharing economy continues to grow, its long-term impact remains uncertain. Some experts believe that it will eventually become a core pillar of the global economy, as more industries adopt decentralized and peer-to-peer models. Others argue that traditional businesses, strengthened by updated laws and consumer protections, may reclaim lost ground. What is clear is that the sharing economy has already transformed how people work, travel, and live—and it will continue to pose challenges for governments, companies, and consumers alike.
